What's the math behind this?

The assignment problem

We have some taxis and the same number of passengers, and each taxi picks up exactly one person. Among all the ways to pair them up, we want the one where the taxis drive the least distance in total.

Why not just send each taxi to the nearest passenger?

Because the nearest choices can collide, and fixing one collision can cost a lot somewhere else. Picture one long street with taxi A at block 2, taxi B at block 4, and passengers at blocks 0 and 3. The passenger at block 3 is the nearest one for both taxis. If A takes them (1 block), B has to drive 4 blocks to the other passenger: 5 blocks in total. If A instead drives 2 blocks to the other passenger, B needs only 1 block: just 3 in total. The best plan isn't always best for each taxi on its own; you have to look at everyone together.

Taxicab distance

Taxis can't fly in straight lines; they follow the streets. On a grid, the distance between two points is how far apart they are left-to-right plus how far apart they are up-and-down: |x₁ − x₂| + |y₁ − y₂|. This is the taxicab metric. Here it's measured in city blocks, the distance between two neighbouring streets. One wrinkle: if two people are in the middle of the same block on parallel streets, the taxi must first drive to a corner, so the real route is a little longer than the formula says. This site always uses the real route.

As a formula

Write t₁, …, tₙ for the taxis and p₁, …, pₙ for the passengers. A pairing is a permutation σ: taxi i picks up passenger σ(i). We want to

minimize  ∑i d(ti, pσ(i))  over all permutations σ,

where d is the driving distance.

How the computer solves it

There are n! possible pairings: 120 for 5 taxis, 3,628,800 for 10, and over 2 quintillion for 20. Trying them all quickly becomes hopeless. Instead, assignment algorithms (the Hungarian algorithm, and the Jonker–Volgenant method this site uses, which is the same one as in the SciPy library) build the best pairing step by step. Along the way they keep a set of "prices" that prove no better pairing exists. They're guaranteed to find the true optimum in roughly n³ steps, without checking all n! pairings.

A tropical connection

In tropical (min-plus) arithmetic, "adding" two numbers means taking the smaller one, and "multiplying" means ordinary addition. Rewrite the formula for a determinant with these operations and you get exactly the minimum above: the optimal total is the tropical determinant of the table of distances. That's a lovely link between this puzzle and tropical geometry, but this site computes it with the classical algorithm described above.
